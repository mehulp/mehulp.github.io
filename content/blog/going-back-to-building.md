I've spent over 22 years in IT, and for about the first 15 of them I wrote code regularly. I started with mainframes, moved into Big Data, and spent much of my career figuring out why something wasn't working the way it should.

Then, gradually, I moved into engineering management.

It wasn't a sudden change. First came the Technical Scrum Master role, then managing a team, then multiple teams. Before I knew it, my days were filled with planning, reviews, meetings, production issues, hiring and everything else that comes with managing engineering teams.

I enjoyed that part of my career, and I still do. Working with engineers, helping them grow and seeing a team deliver something meaningful has its own satisfaction.

But somewhere along the way, I stopped building things myself.

## Missing the Joy of Building

As an engineering manager, I was still involved in technical discussions, reviewing designs, challenging assumptions and helping teams solve problems.

But it's not the same as sitting down with an idea and making it work.

There's a different kind of satisfaction in writing code, seeing it fail, figuring out why, and finally getting it to work.

I realised I missed that.

The development world had also changed considerably. Technologies I knew had evolved beyond recognition, new frameworks had appeared, and the way people built and deployed applications was different.

I wanted to get comfortable with that world again.

## Starting Again

Initially, it felt a little strange.

After years of discussing architecture and reviewing technical approaches, I was now searching for basic syntax, debugging configuration issues and wondering why an application wouldn't start.

Some things came back naturally. Others took time.

Rather than following tutorials endlessly, I decided to build applications around problems I understood or wanted to explore.

That's how Study Hub, InterviewPal and Job Search Copilot became part of my learning.

Building them meant working through APIs, data models, frontend behaviour, deployment, testing and details that are easy to overlook when looking at a system from a distance.

It reminded me how much effort goes into turning even a simple idea into something reliable.

## Building With AI

One big difference this time around has been AI.

I've been using ChatGPT and Claude regularly to understand unfamiliar code, explore approaches, generate initial implementations and troubleshoot issues.

Things that would earlier have taken days can now be done much faster.

But I've also learnt not to accept everything these tools produce.

Code can look reasonable and still have problems. An application might work for the happy path but fail when something unexpected happens. Sometimes, the suggested architecture is more complicated than necessary.

And sometimes, fixing AI-generated code takes longer than expected.

I've found that the better I understand the problem, the better I can use these tools. Design choices, trade-offs, security, testing and checking whether a solution makes sense still matter.

AI helps me build faster. It doesn't remove the need to understand what I'm building.

## Looking at Engineering Differently

Getting back into development has also changed how I look at engineering management.

It's easy to discuss timelines, estimates and technical complexity in meetings. It's different when you're actually working through something that looked straightforward but turns out to have several complications.

I'd experienced this before as a developer, but these projects have brought that perspective back.

They've reminded me why engineers need uninterrupted time, why requirements are rarely as clear as they seem, and why getting something working isn't the same as making it ready for others.

I think this is helping me become a better engineering leader, not because I'm writing more code, but because I'm staying closer to the work and its challenges.

## Still Learning

I'm not trying to go back to being the programmer I used to be. Technology has changed, and so have I.

I still enjoy engineering leadership, but I also want to keep learning, experimenting and understanding how modern software is built.

Some projects will work out well. Others might remain half-finished experiments. I'm fine with that.

For now, I'm enjoying taking an idea, building something around it, getting stuck occasionally and learning along the way.

And that's what this website is about. A place to share what I'm building, what I'm learning and the things I figure out along the way.
